# Plus One — Buddy / Companion Side: Deeper Research

Scope: what solo concertgoers need when considering joining strangers, and what makes stranger meetups work or fail. New evidence only (excludes Bandsintown 2022, viagogo UK 2024, Ticketmaster 2025, Eventbrite 2025, Deloitte 2024, Cigna/Gallup/Pew loneliness, BMJ 2025, Timeleft/Pie/222 traction, Pew dating-app safety).
Compiled 2026-09-28. NA = North America. Non-NA sources labeled.

---

## 1. Solo consumption psychology: why people avoid going alone

### Findings
- **Ratner & Hamilton (2015), "Inhibited from Bowling Alone," Journal of Consumer Research 42(2):266–283.** People are inhibited from public hedonic activities (e.g., a movie) alone because they anticipate others will infer they have few social connections; they also overestimate how much companionship is needed for enjoyment. Reframing the activity as more utilitarian, or reducing the number of observers, raised willingness to go alone. Exact effect sizes were not accessible (paywalled). https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/42/2/266/1816188
  - Field study (Univ. of Maryland, art gallery, 2013–14): before going in, people expected to enjoy it less alone than with a friend. On exit, they had "enjoyed it as much alone." (Ratner, quoted in Behavioral Scientist) https://behavioralscientist.org/fear-of-flying-solo-dont-panic-there-are-benefits-to-venturing-out-alone/ ; also https://msb.georgetown.edu/news-story/value-going-alone/
  - Private activities and utilitarian framings (learning at a museum) feel acceptable alone; visible hedonic activities do not.
- **Jurewicz, Goode & Kristofferson (2025), "Why Are You Bowling Alone?", Journal of the Association for Consumer Research 10(4), published Sept 2, 2025.** Solo experiences make people less happy partly because being alone feels less chosen. https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/737205
  - Study 1 (N=605): happiness solo M=3.99 vs social M=4.19; perceived autonomy solo 14.57 vs social 21.45.
  - Study 2 (N=200, going to a movie): anticipated happiness solo 4.81 vs social 5.81.
  - Study 3 (N=411): solitude framed as chosen raised happiness (5.41 vs 4.84 unchosen).
  - Study 4 (N=568): framing going solo as common narrowed the gap (alone 5.75 vs social 5.99).
- **Boothby, Clark & Bargh (2014), "Shared Experiences Are Amplified," Psychological Science 25:2209–2216.** Tasting chocolate at the same time as another person (a confederate; no talking) made pleasant chocolate taste better and bitter chocolate taste worse. Participants felt more absorbed and "more in tune" with the other person. Study 1: 23 female college students (US, Yale). https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797614551162 ; https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/10/141007103433.htm
  - Implication: a stranger physically co-attending amplifies a good experience. Sharing does not require friendship or conversation.
- **Wu, Hamilton, Kim & Ratner (2020 working paper, Wharton), "When Are Shared Experiences Less Enjoyable Than Solo Experiences?"** With a partner whose interests are unclear, enjoyment of a shared museum visit was lower than going solo (M=3.54 vs 4.01, p=.030). With clear partner interests, shared ≈ solo (4.28 vs 4.01, n.s.). The cost comes from coordinating with the partner. The authors note concerts and movies have low interdependence because pacing is fixed, so the cost is smaller there. https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/When-Are-Shared-Experiences-Less-Enjoyable-Than-Solo-Experiences_draft.pdf
- **Tschacher et al. (2023), Scientific Reports, "Audience synchronies in live concerts…"** (non-NA: Berlin; N=132 at three classical concerts). Audiences showed clear physiological synchrony (heart rate, breathing, skin conductance) and movement synchrony. Heart-rate synchrony rose when listeners felt moved. Electrodermal synchrony was **lower** among listeners who valued the social side of concerts (being in company, meeting friends). https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-41960-2
- **Epley & Schroeder (2014), "Mistakenly Seeking Solitude," J. Experimental Psychology: General** (Chicago trains and buses, NA). Commuters told to talk to a stranger had a happier ride than those who stayed quiet, the opposite of what people predicted. People wrongly assume others don't want to talk. Happiness did not depend on who they talked to. https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/talk-to-a-stranger-itll-make-you-happier
- **Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom & Clark (2018), "The Liking Gap," Psychological Science.** After talking, people underestimate how much their partner liked them. The gap held across conversation lengths and lasted months among new dorm mates (US). https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797618783714
- Solo behavior is becoming normal (NA): **OpenTable solo dining reservations +23% YoY** (US, June 2025–June 2026 vs prior year; Thursday solo +25%; avg solo spend $94, +7%). Axios, July 17, 2026. https://www.axios.com/2026/07/17/solo-dining-reservations-jump-opentable

### Design implications
- The barrier is fear of being seen alone, more than actual enjoyment. The crew mainly works as social cover, so present joining a crew as "going with people," never as "a lonely person seeking friends."
- The Jurewicz framing results suggest positioning solo as a choice, with lines like "Going solo by choice, crew optional." Showing that solo attendance is common (e.g., "412 solo fans going to this show") narrows the happiness gap.
- Amplification works with silent co-presence. A crew can just stand together, so "meet at the door and enjoy the show" should be a valid minimum commitment.
- Clear shared interests remove the coordination cost. Crew matching should show how members like to experience the show: pit or seated, sing-along or quiet, stay for the encore, pre-drinks or not. Mismatched partners can be worse than going alone.
- Tschacher suggests too much socializing can distract from the music. Keep the social part before and after the show and let people focus on the music during it.
- The liking gap and the Epley results support light prompts after the show, such as "Tell your crew you'd go again," because people underestimate how much others liked them.

### Gaps
- Exact effect sizes for Ratner & Hamilton (paywalled). No concert-specific study compares enjoyment of attending solo, with a friend, and with a crew of strangers.

## 2. Flakiness / no-shows with free vs committed meetups

### Findings
- **Meetup (official blog, 2020, updated Aug 2023):** typical RSVP-to-attendance is **40–50% for free events** and **70–85% with an event fee**. https://www.meetup.com/blog/how-to-get-people-to-show-up-to-your-events/
- **Industry aggregate (Nunify, citing Event Tech Live, year unspecified):** free events lose 40–60% of registrants; paid events see 90–97% attendance. Treat as directional. https://www.nunify.com/blogs/event-attendance-rate
- **37signals/Basecamp "Breakfast with Basecamp," Chicago (Skift Meetings, Sept 2, 2026, NA):** the free event had 84 registrants and 38 attendees (55% no-show). A **$100 deposit refundable at the door** brought 55 registrants and 50 attendees (**10% no-show**). Registrations fell 35%, actual attendance rose 32%. Jason Fried: "when you pay for something you value it more." https://meetings.skift.com/2026/09/02/the-100-experiment-that-slashed-no-show-rates-to-just-10/
- Timeleft model (company blog): prepaid subscription of about $20/month, groups of 4–6, vetted public venues. Timeleft publishes no no-show rate. https://timeleft.com/blog/timeleft-vs-meetup/

### Design implications
- Plus One has a built-in commitment device because everyone in the crew already holds a ticket. That is a paid, sunk-cost commitment, which is structurally closer to the 70–97% attendance band than to the 40–50% band for free events.
- The weak point is the pre-show meetup, which is free. Consider a small refundable "show-up hold," or reputation signals ("showed up 5/5 meetups"), and a day-of check-in ("I'm here" at the meet spot).
- Plan for 1–2 dropouts. A crew of up to 6 still works if 2 flake, while a 1:1 buddy fails completely. This is another reason to use groups rather than pairs.
- Tie reliability to the ticket-swap side. A user who flakes on a crew loses trust score used for face-value swaps, which links the two halves of the product.

### Gaps
- No public data on no-show rates for concert-buddy or friendship apps (Bumble BFF, Timeleft). No audited source for the "Event Tech Live" figures.

## 3. Friendship formation: time, repetition, where adults meet friends

### Findings
- **Hall (2019; online 2018), J. Social and Personal Relationships, "How many hours does it take to make a friend?"** (Univ. of Kansas, NA). About **50 hours** to go from acquaintance to casual friend (40–60), about **90 hours** to friend (80–100), and **200+ hours** to close friend. Study 1: N=355 adults who had moved in the past 6 months. Study 2: N=112 KU freshmen surveyed at weeks 4 and 7. Leisure time ("hanging out, joking around") counted; work time counted less. https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend ; https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265407518761225
- **Survey Center on American Life, "State of American Friendship" (May 14–23, 2021; N=2,019; Ipsos KnowledgePanel; ±2.4 pts):** 12% of Americans have no close friends (3% in 1990). **46% made a new friend in the past 12 months** (young adults 56%, seniors 41%). Where close friends were met: work 54%, school 47%, through existing friends 40%, neighborhood 35%, place of worship 21%, **clubs/organizations 19%**, online 8%. https://www.americansurveycenter.org/research/the-state-of-american-friendship-change-challenges-and-loss/
- **Pew Research (July 17–23, 2023; N=5,057 US adults, ATP):** 8% have no close friends, 53% have 1–4, 38% have 5+. Under-30s are least likely to have 5+ (32% vs 49% of 65+). 61% say close friends are extremely or very important for a fulfilling life. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/10/12/what-does-friendship-look-like-in-america/
- **Bumble (Feb 2023; 1,000+ US adults, company-commissioned):** 67% of Gen Z respondents said making friends online lessened their loneliness. https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20230726935186/en/

### Design implications
- One concert is roughly 4–6 hours together, about 10% of the 50 hours needed for a casual friend. Friendship comes from repeating shows. The product should reward re-crewing: "Your crew from the Phoebe Bridgers show is also following Boygenius. Go again?" Keep crews as persistent objects after the show.
- Hall's finding that leisure time counts most suits concerts, which are pure leisure. Pre-show dinner and the post-show debrief add high-quality hours.
- Only 8% met close friends online and 19% through clubs. Adults mostly make friends through repeated structured settings (work, school). A crew that goes to shows together regularly can act like a club.
- Under-30s have the fewest close friends (Pew), which matches the likely core user.

### Gaps
- No data on how many concert-met acquaintances become friends. The Survey Center item has no separate "concerts/events" category.

## 4. Safety when meeting strangers for platonic purposes

### Findings
- **Our Music My Body (Chicago, NA; N=509 music fans; Nov 27–Dec 11, 2017):** **92% of female fans** reported harassment at shows. 84% of male-identified LGBTQ+ fans experienced physical and non-physical harassment. Fans want more security measures (99%), staff trained in violence prevention (84%), venues that don't book artists with abuse histories (80%), clear anti-harassment signage (75%), and venue partnerships with prevention organizations (67%). A respondent stressed bystanders "prepared to intervene." https://www.ourresilience.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/OurMusicMyBody_2017SurveyReport.pdf
- Bumble For Friends (2023 launch): group chats, photo-and-interest profiles, and matching before messaging. The press release lists no dedicated safety tools. https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20230726935186/en/
- Timeleft: algorithm-matched groups of 4–6 at vetted public venues, with location revealed only hours before. https://timeleft.com/blog/timeleft-vs-meetup/ ; Vice review: https://www.vice.com/en/article/timeleft-friend-finding-platform-review/
- (Related, non-NA, same viagogo UK 2024 study already on file: 7 in 10 UK women have never gone to a concert alone. Nearly 40% felt more self-confident after their first solo gig. https://www.stylist.co.uk/entertainment/music/concerts-alone-womens-safety/886055)

### Design implications
- In OMMB, fans named bystanders willing to intervene as key. A crew is a group of committed bystanders. Frame the crew as having "people who've got your back in the pit," with crew norms (a check-in before leaving, nobody leaves alone).
- Minimum safety features: ID or photo verification, meetups only at a public pre-show spot (venue entrance or bar), live location shared within the crew for the show window only, and optional women-only or LGBTQ+-only crews. Add a one-tap report or exit that notifies venue security. Connecting to venue programs (e.g., OMMB-trained venues) is a differentiator.
- Groups spread the risk of 1:1 meetings. Default to crews of 3–6 and avoid pushing 1:1 buddies.

### Gaps
- No quantitative survey found on women's safety perceptions for platonic friend apps specifically, or on which safety features they rank highest. Bumble, Timeleft, and VINA publish no safety-incident or verification-uptake stats. The US has no national equivalent of UK Safe Gigs for Women figures. OMMB 2017 is dated.

## 5. Concert-specific stranger bonding

### Findings
- **Harris Poll, "State of Taylor Swift" (Mar 22–24, 2024; N=2,043 US adults, NA):** **80% of Swifties** say they've formed deep friendships from a shared Taylor Swift connection (45% of casual fans). 65% connect with other fans on social media. 82% feel understood around other Swift fans. **87% of Eras concert attendees (n=319)** called it "otherworldly" because of connection with others. https://theharrispoll.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/State-of-Taylor-Swift-April-2024.pdf
- **Live Nation "Living for Live" (Nov 11, 2025; 40,000 people aged 18–54, 15 countries; global, not NA-specific):** nearly 80% say concerts bring their family closer. 93% attend because they crave real over virtual experiences. https://www.marketscreener.com/news/global-report-surveying-40-000-people-finds-live-music-is-the-world-s-favorite-form-of-entertainment-ce7d5fd9de88f220
- Community-driven meetups (non-NA, UK: The Lonely Girls Club, 60,000+ Facebook members, member-organized outings including theater). The founder says getting past the "terror barrier" of messaging after the first meeting is what makes friendships stick. https://www.vice.com/en/article/timeleft-friend-finding-platform-review/
- Stanford CS147 (2024) student concept "ConcertBuds" shows the problem is recognized in design. https://stanford.edu/class/cs147/projects24/Designing-for-Movement/ConcertBuds

### Design implications
- Stranger bonding happens most in fandoms with rituals (bracelet trading, dress codes, GA lines). Build crew rituals: a shared crew name or emoji, an outfit theme, a pre-show "setlist bets" card, and a bracelet or merch swap prompt.
- The 80% vs 45% gap between committed and casual fans suggests matching on depth of fandom (albums known, shows attended) matters as much as matching on the same show.
- Post-show follow-up is where friendships stick or die. Auto-create a crew memory (photos, setlist) and prompt the next show.

### Gaps
- No rigorous survey of "% of concertgoers who made a friend at a show." No data on how many members concert-buddy subreddits or tour Discords have, or how active they are (not verified in this pass). Tips from solo attendees, such as arriving early and GA-line friendships, are anecdotal only.

## 6. Group size and format

### Findings
- **Krems & Wilkes (2019), Evolution and Human Behavior, "Why are conversations limited to about four people?"** Conversations of 5+ speakers tend to split into smaller groups. Most adults can track about three other minds, which fits a four-person conversation. Only about 20% manage higher-order intentionality. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1090513818301491
- **Social Connection Guidelines evidence brief (Canada, NA):** from the Canadian Social Connection Survey 2022, median gathering size is 5 (IQR 3–9). Enjoyment dips toward 4, rises from 5 to 8, and falls above 8. Emotional closeness drops as groups grow (p=.032). Above 5 people, conversation shifts from dialogue to monologue (Fay & Carletta 2000). 63% prefer weekly 1:1 time with friends vs 29% for group gatherings. Older finding (Calsyn & Becker 1976): chance of someone leaving was 34.8% for groups of 2 and 85.7% for groups of 4. https://www.socialconnectionguidelines.org/en/evidence-briefs/what-is-the-ideal-size-of-a-get-together
- Timeleft (4–6 per table) and Vice's reviewer (6 strangers) found forced intimacy awkward, with strangers jumping into fertility and breakups. The format can fail when conversation is the only activity. https://www.vice.com/en/article/timeleft-friend-finding-platform-review/

### Design implications
- A cap of 6 is justified. Enjoyment peaks around 5–8 and falls above 8, and a 6-person crew naturally splits into two conversations of 3, which also covers dropouts. A 4–6 target with a minimum of 3 is defensible.
- Concerts fix the forced-intimacy problem that dinner-with-strangers formats have. The show is the main activity and conversation is optional, which also matches the Eventbrite 2025 preference already on file.
- Evidence that people prefer 1:1 with existing friends does not transfer to strangers. For strangers, groups spread the awkwardness and the risk.

### Gaps
- No direct experiment comparing 1:1 and small-group formats for perceived safety or awkwardness among strangers. The groups-feel-safer claim rests on inference and product practice (Timeleft, 222), not measurement.

## Top 10 data points
1. Meetup: free-event RSVP show-up is 40–50% vs 70–85% with a fee (Meetup blog, 2020/2023).
2. $100 refundable deposit cut no-shows from 55% to 10% (37signals Chicago, Skift, Sept 2026).
3. About 50 / 90 / 200+ hours to become a casual / regular / close friend (Hall, JSPR 2018/2019; N=355 + 112).
4. 46% of Americans made a new friend in the past year; only 19% met close friends through clubs and 8% online (Survey Center on American Life, May 2021, N=2,019).
5. 80% of Swifties formed deep friendships through the fandom; 87% of Eras attendees (n=319) called it otherworldly because of connection with others (Harris Poll, Mar 2024, N=2,043).
6. 92% of female fans reported harassment at shows, and bystanders willing to intervene were named as key (Our Music My Body, Chicago, 2017, N=509).
7. Solo movie-going: anticipated happiness 4.81 vs 5.81 with others. Framing solo as chosen or common narrows the gap (Jurewicz et al., JACR, Sept 2025; N=200–605 per study).
8. People expected to enjoy a gallery less alone but enjoyed it as much. Fear of looking friendless drives avoidance (Ratner & Hamilton, JCR 2015).
9. With a partner whose interests are unclear, shared visits were less enjoyable than solo (3.54 vs 4.01) (Wu, Hamilton, Kim & Ratner, 2020).
10. Conversations cap at about 4 people; Canadian gatherings have a median of 5 people, and enjoyment falls above 8 (Krems & Wilkes 2019; Canadian Social Connection Survey 2022).
Honorable mention: OpenTable solo dining reservations +23% YoY in the US (Axios, July 2026). Shared experiences are amplified even without talking (Boothby et al., Psych Science 2014).
